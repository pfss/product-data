# Content Format Capital Allocation — Q3 Read

**From:** Pedro — prepared as a work sample for *Manager, Product Finance & Strategy, Product Merchandising*
**To:** Hiring team
**Re:** Where should the next dollar of incremental content-format investment go — Games, Live, or Podcasts?

*Note: this memo uses an illustrative model I built for this application, not real Netflix data. It's meant to demonstrate the reasoning and format, not to claim insider numbers. The interactive version of the underlying model is linked at the bottom.*

---

### The decision in one line

Weight the next quarter's incremental content-format pool toward **Live**, hold **Podcasts** as the capital-efficient complement, and treat **Games** as the swing lever once we have one more quarter of engagement data.

### Why

None of these three formats has a mature benchmark inside our existing content economics — that's the whole problem this role exists to solve. So instead of comparing them on a single number, I modeled each on the same three primitives: what it costs to produce an hour of content, how many member-hours that hour generates once it's live, and what it's worth in retention terms. That gives two comparable outputs per format — **cost per member-hour** (efficiency) and an **ROI index** (retention value per dollar) — without pretending we have more certainty than we do.

Under the assumptions I used:

- **Live** carries the highest production cost per hour, but its concentrated, appointment-viewing nature drives the highest retention lift per dollar spent. It's the format most likely to move the retention number a board would actually ask about.
- **Podcasts** is the cheapest way to generate raw member-hours by a wide margin. It won't move retention much on its own, but it's a low-risk way to keep the content slate broad while we learn more about the other two.
- **Games** sits in the middle on both dimensions. The honest answer is that its member-hour multiplier is the assumption I have the least confidence in — replay value is real but hard to size without a longer observation window.

### What I'd want to be wrong about

The model is only as good as three inputs per format, and I'd flag the games engagement multiplier as the one worth stress-testing first — it's the number most likely to change the recommendation if it moves. That's a deliberate choice: better to be transparent about which assumption carries the most risk than to present false precision.

### The ask

Fund Live at the higher end of its range this quarter, keep Podcasts flat as the efficient complement, and revisit Games once we have another quarter of title-level engagement data to replace the current placeholder assumption.
